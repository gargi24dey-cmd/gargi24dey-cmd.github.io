Inside the U.S. Congress

Representation, Performance & Power

This project explores the story of congressional elections and political power in the United States using congressional data from 1973 to 2018. Rather than treating elections only as a question of who won or lost, it follows the broader journey from the ballot box to political power — examining electoral performance, party composition, demographic representation, seniority, and congressional leadership. The analysis combines data visualization with statistical modelling to identify how electoral performance relates to party affiliation, demographics, majority-party status, and seniority.

Key idea: elections determine who enters Congress, party balance determines who forms the majority, and institutional experience and leadership structures help shape political power.

Research Questions

  • How has congressional representation changed over time?
  • How are party affiliation, demographic characteristics, majority-party status, and seniority associated with electoral performance?
  • How are these characteristics associated with majority-party membership?

Dataset

The dataset covers 1973–2018, containing 10,262 observations and 38 variables, including year, state, party affiliation, gender, African American and Latino representation, electoral vote percentage, majority-party status, seniority, political power indicators, and formal leadership positions (Speaker, committee chair, subcommittee chair, majority/minority leader). The statistical models use observations with complete information for the variables included in each model.

Project Story

The analysis is structured as a journey from elections to institutional power:

  • Who is represented? — changes in demographic representation over time
  • Who controls Congress? — party composition and the Democratic–Republican balance
  • How well do individual members perform? — electoral vote percentages compared across parties and demographic groups
  • What happens after election? — seniority, political power, and leadership
  • What does the statistical evidence show? — two regression models examining these relationships systematically

Methods

Data Visualization

Built using ggplot2 and gganimate in R, including animated visualizations of demographic representation, party composition, electoral performance, seniority, political power, and leadership over time.

OLS Regression

Examines the association between electoral vote percentage and party affiliation, gender, African American representation, Latino representation, majority-party status, and seniority. The model explains approximately 12.7% of the variation in vote share. Since heteroskedasticity was detected, robust standard errors were used for inference.

Logistic Regression

Examines the association between majority-party membership and party affiliation, gender, African American and Latino representation, seniority, and electoral vote percentage. Classification accuracy is approximately 59.8%, indicating the included characteristics carry some predictive information but aren't sufficient on their own to accurately predict majority-party membership.

Key Findings

  • Congressional representation changes substantially over time
  • The Democratic–Republican balance shifts across congressional periods
  • Electoral performance varies considerably between individual members
  • Party affiliation, demographic characteristics, majority-party status, and seniority are statistically associated with vote percentage
  • Seniority and political power show a relationship within Congress, though this is exploratory rather than causal
  • Political power and formal leadership positions are concentrated among a relatively small share of members
  • Majority-party membership is associated with several of the characteristics examined

Main Conclusion

The project tells a broader story than simply asking who won an election. Elections determine who enters Congress; party balance determines who forms the majority; experience and institutional structures shape political influence, while leadership positions represent the concentration of that influence. The findings should be read as associations rather than causal effects — factors such as district characteristics, opposing candidates, campaign resources, and incumbency are not fully captured by the models.
